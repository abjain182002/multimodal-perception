# Exploratory robotic-arm handling pilot

A supplementary CPU simulation examined a downstream requirement beyond object detection: adapting grip effort during contact. A Panda arm moved abstract rigid components from a nominal grasp location to a placement target. Three profiles varied in mass, size and friction, with five initial XY offsets paired across three control policies. These parameters were assumed rather than measured from factory parts. The 45-trial protocol was fixed after a separate development-profile motion check and before evaluation of the three profiles.

All policies shared the same arm trajectory, approach, grasp and placement geometry. Two baselines maintained per-finger motor limits of 3 N and 20 N. The feedback policy began at 3 N and increased its limit in 2 N steps when simulator-derived relative height indicated downward movement, up to 20 N. This is an ideal state-feedback policy: it does not use the dissertation's object detectors or reproduce a physical tactile sensor. The objects were held through simulated contact and friction without attachment constraints.

The low fixed limit completed 10 of 15 placements, whereas the high fixed limit and feedback policy each completed 15 of 15. All five low-limit failures concerned the heavy-component profile. For that profile, the mean sum of simulated finger-object normal forces was 38.63 N with the high fixed limit and 16.08 N with feedback, a reduction of 58.36% while retaining five successful placements. The light and narrow profiles required no increase from the initial feedback limit. This demonstrates adaptation within the chosen simulator conditions; it does not establish a generally optimal controller. In particular, an intermediate fixed limit has not been tested and might achieve a similar outcome.

Success combined sustained lift and transfer with a final position tolerance of 30 mm, height tolerance of 10 mm and linear-speed threshold of 0.02 m/s. Mean endpoint XY errors for successful profile-policy groups were approximately 13–14 mm. A post-hoc 10 mm tolerance check yields no successful placements; the systematic endpoint offset remains unexplained. Consequently, the demonstration concerns coarse handling rather than precision insertion or assembly. Motor commands and contact-force measurements are distinct: reported loading sums contacts across both fingers, while limits apply separately to each motor. Failed trials remain included in loading averages, so a low force value cannot be interpreted favourably without its task outcome.

The experiment provides a concrete link between perception and action requirements. A perception system can supply object pose information, but successful handling also depends on interaction feedback. The current experiment supplies that information from simulator state, leaving visual pose estimation and tactile observation errors untested. It therefore extends the dissertation with an exploratory control example, rather than demonstrating end-to-end camera–LiDAR manipulation. Rigid objects provide no evidence of damage reduction, and five position perturbations per profile do not establish real-world reliability. The result motivates testing observation uncertainty and a stronger fixed-limit baseline before any claim of general advantage.
